> This is a proposed detailed structure for the reference work *TRANSFORMATIONAL VALUE — Understanding, Designing and Realizing Value in Changing Systems*. It is a content-design proposal, not approved final contents and not a claim that the underlying scientific or methodological propositions are validated. Chapter names and sequence may change after review of source assets and content gaps.

---

## 1. Editorial logic

The book should take the reader through three connected but distinct questions:

1. **Understanding Transformation:** What is a system, what transformations can it perform, under what conditions, and how do its accessible possibilities and trajectories change?
2. **Designing Value:** How can people understand a system and its context, identify opportunities, connect knowledge and evidence, design alternatives, make decisions, act and learn?
3. **Evolving Value:** How can results be evaluated, learning institutionalised, practices governed and the framework and its supporting ecosystem evolve responsibly?

The structure deliberately avoids treating transformation as synonymous with value. Part I establishes the systems and transformation perspective; Part II introduces VALUE as an applied framework; Part III addresses evaluation, governance and evolution.

## 2. Intended reader journey

- **Orientation:** understand the purpose, vocabulary and boundaries of the book.
- **Conceptual foundation:** acquire a systems view of transformation and accessible possibilities.
- **Applied method:** learn VALUE as a repeatable but context-sensitive intervention framework.
- **Evaluation and evolution:** understand evidence, limits, learning, governance and future development.
- **Reference use:** return to specific chapters, glossary entries, artefacts and references as needed.

Each chapter should be independently addressable in Learning while retaining a coherent reading sequence.

## 3. Recommended chapter template

Each chapter should use a consistent but adaptable pattern:

1. **Chapter purpose:** why the chapter exists and where it fits.
2. **Learning objectives:** what the reader should understand or be able to do.
3. **Opening question or situation:** a motivating context, not a fabricated claim.
4. **Core explanation:** concepts and reasoning, introduced progressively.
5. **Key distinctions:** terms or ideas that must not be conflated.
6. **Diagram or model:** where it materially improves understanding.
7. **Worked example:** labelled as real, anonymised, synthetic or fictional.
8. **Limitations and common misunderstandings:** what the chapter does not establish.
9. **Summary:** key ideas to retain.
10. **Reflection / application questions:** prompts to test understanding.
11. **Related chapters and glossary:** navigable cross-links.
12. **References and source notes:** traceable support for substantive claims.

Not every chapter needs all twelve elements. The template is a quality guide, not a rigid form.

## 4. Cross-cutting editorial requirements

### 4.1 Terminology discipline
- Introduce a term before using it as if already understood.
- Use one preferred definition for each canonical concept.
- Explain differences between related terms.
- Keep acronyms to a minimum and expand them on first use.
- Do not use CCV and IVS as if they were validated measures.

### 4.2 Evidence discipline
- Label the epistemic status of substantive TGCV claims.
- Distinguish external knowledge from TGCV proposals and VALUE methodology.
- Identify the scope of each empirical result.
- Mark fictional and synthetic examples visibly.
- Never infer causality from sequence, association or a demonstration alone.

### 4.3 Learning design
- Give each chapter explicit learning objectives.
- Use examples to teach a concept, not to imply proof.
- Include questions that encourage application and critical reasoning.
- Link concepts to their canonical glossary pages.
- Make chapter pages useful independently as well as in sequence.

### 4.4 Bilingual publication
- Maintain equivalent Spanish and English chapter structures.
- Preserve approved proper names and technical labels.
- Review translations for conceptual equivalence, not just literal wording.
- Record language completeness and review status.

## 6. Structural questions for review

These questions should be resolved before treating the contents as approved:

1. **Part I depth:** Is six chapters the right balance, or should the TGCV research programme, models and empirical evidence have a separate chapter rather than being integrated into the relevant conceptual chapters and the research guide?
2. **TGCV technical depth:** Which mathematical, formal or computational material belongs in the main narrative, and which belongs in advanced sections or supplementary technical notes?
3. **Part II teaching balance:** Should the VALUE lifecycle be taught phase-by-phase as proposed, or should a complete worked case be interleaved to make the method easier to understand?
4. **VSM placement:** Is a dedicated CONNECT chapter the right home for the VSM, with later chapters referring back to it?
5. **Evidence chapter:** Should Chapter 13 remain a cross-cutting chapter between DESIGN and DECIDE, or should evidence be taught as a recurring thread in every lifecycle phase with a shorter dedicated reference chapter?
6. **CCV and IVS:** Is one combined chapter sufficient, given that IVS is exploratory and not a required part of the lifecycle?
7. **Part III scope:** Should governance, community and standardisation remain in the main book, or should some of this material be reserved for a separately maintained governance guide?
8. **ValueLabOS:** How much should the book say about the ecosystem before product capabilities are sufficiently specified and demonstrated?
9. **Worked case:** Should the fictional clinic case be a continuous thread across Part II, a single complete chapter, or an appendix?
10. **Audience and technical level:** Should the main narrative target technically literate practitioners, with advanced research notes for specialist readers?
