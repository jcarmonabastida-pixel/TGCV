# Thinking in Systems

## 1.1. What Do We Mean by a System?

### From a collection of parts to an organised whole

When we encounter a complex installation or service, it is tempting to begin by listing its components. That inventory is useful, but it does not yet tell us how the system works.

Take a municipal water supply network. Its physical components may include reservoirs, treatment facilities, pumping stations, pipes, valves and delivery points. Knowing that these components exist does not explain how water reaches a particular neighbourhood, how the network copes with a peak in demand, or why a failure in one location can disrupt service elsewhere. Those questions require us to examine how the components are arranged and how they depend on one another.

A system is therefore more than a collection of things that happen to be present in the same place. It is an organised whole whose elements are connected in ways that matter to the behaviour we want to understand.

### A working definition

For the purposes of this book, we will use the following working definition:

> A system is an analytical unit comprising organised and related elements, whose behaviour is examined in terms of its configuration and the conditions under which it operates.

Three ideas are central to this definition:

- **Elements** are the components, entities or parts identified as belonging to the system.
- **Organisation** describes how those elements are arranged and coordinated.
- **Relationships** are the connections and dependencies through which elements affect one another and contribute to the operation of the whole.

This is a practical definition rather than an attempt to settle every theoretical debate about systems. It gives us a consistent starting point for examining real installations, infrastructures, services and socio-technical arrangements.

### Why the whole matters

The behaviour of a system cannot always be understood by examining each component in isolation and then adding the results together. The way components are connected can create constraints, dependencies and effects that are visible only at the level of the whole.

In a water network, for instance, the ability to maintain pressure and deliver the required flow depends not only on the capacity of individual pumps or the dimensions of particular pipes. It also depends on network topology, the location and timing of demand, operating arrangements and the consequences of changes elsewhere in the network.

This does not make component-level analysis irrelevant. On the contrary, component properties often matter greatly. The point is that their significance must be interpreted within the configuration in which they operate.

### Three questions for an initial system description

A useful first description should answer three different questions:

1. Which elements are included?
2. How are those elements organised and connected?
3. What behaviour does the resulting whole exhibit under the conditions being considered?

The first question produces an inventory. The second and third begin to explain how the system operates.

### Key idea

To understand a system, examine its organised configuration and the relationships among its elements—not merely the elements considered separately.

### Reflection

Think of an installation, service or infrastructure you know well. Can its performance be explained by looking at its components one by one? Which connections, dependencies or features of its organisation must also be considered?

## 1.2. Purpose, Boundary, Environment and Context

### Defining the object of analysis

Before analysing a system, we need to decide what exactly we are trying to understand. Physical objects may appear to have obvious limits, but the boundary of an analytical system is not always the same as the edge of a building, a piece of equipment or an organisation.

Return to the water supply network. An investigation into hydraulic performance might focus on reservoirs, pumps, pipes, valves and delivery points. An investigation into service continuity may also need to consider maintenance teams, operating procedures and incident-response arrangements. If the question concerns how demand affects availability, consumers and their patterns of use become important to the analysis.

These are different ways of defining the object of study. The appropriate choice depends on the question being asked.

### Purpose: what is the analysis intended to establish?

The purpose of an analysis states what we want to understand, explain or assess. It guides decisions about the system boundary, the level of detail required and the information that needs to be collected.

We should also be careful when describing the purpose of the system itself. Some systems have explicit objectives established by people or institutions. Others are assigned a function by an external observer, or are studied in terms of effects and patterns that do not imply a single intention.

Even where an organisation has a declared objective, its actual priorities may be more complicated. A water utility may state that reliable supply is its principal aim, while operational decisions also reflect cost limits, energy constraints, regulatory obligations and risk management. These considerations may reinforce or compete with one another.

Finally, the purpose of the analysis is not necessarily the purpose of the system. We may examine a water network to understand its energy consumption, even though its primary service is water distribution. Keeping these two meanings separate prevents the analytical question from being confused with the system's stated function.

### Boundary: what belongs inside the system?

The system boundary identifies the elements and relationships included in the unit of analysis. It is an analytical decision, not always a physical or administrative fact.

For a study of water distribution, we might include treatment and distribution infrastructure but exclude household plumbing. If the purpose is to understand the end-to-end delivery of water as experienced by consumers, the boundary may need to extend further.

A boundary that is too broad can make an investigation unwieldy and obscure the issue under examination. One that is too narrow may exclude dependencies that are essential to explaining the observed behaviour.

The boundary should therefore be justified in relation to the purpose of the analysis and made explicit. Two studies can reach different conclusions because they have included different elements or relationships. That difference does not automatically make either study invalid; it does mean that their findings may apply to different analytical objects.

### Environment: what lies beyond the boundary but matters?

The environment consists of external elements and conditions that are relevant to the system because they influence it or are affected by it.

For a water network, these may include the availability of raw water, weather conditions, electricity supply, regulatory requirements and changes in demand. Such factors may lie outside the chosen system boundary while still having a substantial influence on its operation.

A boundary does not imply isolation. An important part of systems analysis is identifying the exchanges and dependencies that cross it: flows of materials, energy, information, resources, demand, constraints or effects.

### Context: under what circumstances should the system be understood?

Context refers to the circumstances that shape how we frame and interpret the system and its behaviour. Depending on the question, these circumstances may be technical, economic, social, institutional, environmental, historical or temporal.

Environment and context are related, but they serve different analytical purposes. The environment concerns what surrounds the bounded system and interacts with it. Context is the wider set of circumstances through which we decide what is relevant and interpret what we observe.

For example, reduced water availability may be an environmental condition. A prolonged drought, seasonal demand peaks and specific regulatory restrictions provide context for understanding the network's response to that condition.

Not every surrounding circumstance deserves equal attention. The analyst must identify which contextual factors matter to the question and explain why they have been included.

### These choices shape what can be concluded

Purpose, boundary, environment and context are closely connected. The analytical purpose informs the boundary; the boundary distinguishes the system from its environment; and the context helps explain the conditions under which the system is being examined.

These are not preliminary formalities that can be ignored once data collection begins. They affect what evidence is gathered, which relationships are investigated and how far the conclusions can reasonably extend.

A clear system description should therefore state:

- what is being studied and for what analytical purpose;
- which elements and relationships fall within the boundary;
- which external factors are considered relevant;
- the circumstances and time horizon used to interpret behaviour;
- which aspects have been excluded and how those exclusions may limit the findings.

There is no single boundary that is correct for every investigation. There is a boundary that can be made more or less appropriate and defensible for a particular purpose.

### Key idea

Before explaining how a system behaves, make clear what the system includes, why it is being examined and the circumstances in which its behaviour is interpreted.

### Reflection

Choose an installation or service with which you are familiar. Formulate a specific question about its operation and define a boundary suitable for investigating it. Which elements would you include? Which factors would remain in the environment? What contextual circumstances could change the interpretation of your findings?


## 1.3. Elements, States, Properties and Behaviours

### Describing what a system consists of and how it operates

Once we have defined the system under study, the next task is to describe its constituent elements and the features that matter to the question we are investigating. Not every element has the same nature, and not every analysis requires the same level of detail. The description should be shaped by the purpose of the investigation.

A water supply network, for example, may include reservoirs, pumps, pipes, valves, sensors, control equipment and delivery points. Its operation may also depend on less tangible elements, such as operating procedures, control rules and information used to make decisions. In a socio-technical system, people and organisations belong in the description whenever their actions, decisions or responsibilities affect the behaviour being examined.

The aim is not to produce an exhaustive inventory of everything that exists. It is to identify the elements needed to explain the system's relevant behaviour.

### Elements and components

We use the term **elements** for the entities or parts distinguished within a system for analytical purposes. Depending on the system and the level of analysis, these may be physical components, people, teams, organisational units, processes, resources, information or subsystems.

The chosen level of decomposition matters. A pumping station may be treated as a single element when examining the overall distribution network. An investigation of energy use or reliability may require us to distinguish its motors, pumps, sensors, power supply and control equipment.

There is no single decomposition that is appropriate for every investigation. A useful description provides enough detail to answer the question without becoming so granular that it obscures the relationships and behaviour we need to understand.

### States: how the system or an element is at a given time

A **state** describes the condition of a system or one of its elements at a particular time or over a defined interval, using variables relevant to the analysis.

In a water network, the state of a reservoir might be described by its water level; the state of a pump by its operating condition and current operating regime; and the state of a network segment by variables such as pressure and flow rate. Describing the state of the network as a whole requires a selected set of variables that captures the aspects of its configuration and operation relevant to the question.

A state is not necessarily a complete account of everything that exists. It is a selective representation: it includes the information needed to distinguish conditions that may have different consequences for the investigation.

It is therefore important to specify which variables are used, how they are observed or estimated, and when the description applies. Different accounts of the same system may use different variables because they address different questions.

### Properties and variables

**Properties** are characteristics attributed to an element or system that help describe it. Some may remain relatively stable over the period of analysis; others vary over time or with operating conditions.

For example, a pipe's diameter and a pump's rated capacity describe characteristics of the equipment. Pressure, temperature, reservoir level and electrical consumption may vary during operation and may be used as state or monitoring variables, depending on the model and the question.

A property should not be confused with the value observed at a particular moment. A pump's rated capacity is not the same as the flow rate it is delivering now. The former characterises the equipment under specified conditions; the latter describes an operating condition.

In practice, the selection of properties and variables should reflect their relevance, availability and quality. A variable that cannot be measured directly may need to be estimated; a characteristic that appears important may prove unnecessary for a particular question. Such choices should be made explicit whenever they affect the conclusions.

### Behaviours: what the system does and how it changes

**Behaviour** describes how a system or one of its elements operates or evolves under particular conditions. It may be observed as a pattern of operation, a response to a disturbance or a change in variables over time.

In the water network, behaviour might include how pressure changes when demand rises, how the control system responds when reservoir levels fall, or how service is restored after a failure. Understanding these patterns requires more than knowing the nominal properties of individual components: we need to examine how they operate within the network's configuration and actual conditions.

It is useful to distinguish state from behaviour. A state describes a condition; behaviour concerns what happens or how that condition evolves. A pressure reading at one moment provides information about a state. A sequence of readings over time may reveal how the network responds to changes in demand.

### Bringing elements, states and behaviours together

These concepts become useful when considered together. Elements tell us which parts we distinguish; their properties describe relevant characteristics; states represent their condition; and behaviours show how they operate or evolve.

Consider a straightforward situation: water consumption increases in one area. Demand is a relevant condition for the analysis. We can observe changes in flow and pressure, identify which pumps and valves are operating, and examine how the control system responds. Explaining what happens requires us to connect the observed variables with the elements and relationships that make up the network.

The same observation may have different explanations if the configuration changes or if information about relevant elements is missing. Describing a system is therefore not a matter of collecting isolated values. It involves organising information so that the system's operation can be interpreted.

### Deciding what to describe

A rigorous description does not need to include every possible property. It should select the features needed to answer the analytical question and record any omissions that may matter.

When preparing an initial description, we can ask:

- Which elements or subsystems need to be distinguished?
- What level of detail is appropriate?
- Which properties characterise those elements?
- Which variables describe the relevant states?
- Which behaviours need to be observed, and over what period?
- What information is unavailable or outside the scope of the description?

These questions help produce a coherent account and prevent the information currently available from being mistaken for a complete description of the system.

### Key idea

Describing a system requires us to identify its elements, select relevant properties and variables, represent its states and observe its behaviours. None of these dimensions, taken alone, explains how the whole system operates.

### Reflection

Choose an installation or service you know. Identify some of its elements and select one relevant property and one variable for each. Which states could you describe? What behaviour would you need to observe over time to understand how the system operates?


## 1.4. Actors, Capabilities, Resources and Dependencies

### Who is involved, and what do they need?

So far, we have described a system in terms of its elements, states and behaviours. In many real-world systems—particularly those combining technology, people and organisations—we also need to identify who takes part, what they are able to do and which means they depend on.

A water supply network illustrates this well. Its operation requires infrastructure and equipment, but it also relies on operators who monitor the installation, maintenance teams who respond to incidents, managers who make decisions, and organisations that provide energy, materials or specialist services. The physical infrastructure alone cannot explain how the service is sustained.

This perspective extends a component-based description by recognising the contribution of participants and the means that support their activities.

### Actors: participants who can intervene

We use **actors** to refer to people, groups or organisations able to intervene in the system or in aspects of its operation. Their roles may include operating, maintaining, deciding, regulating, supplying or using the system.

In a water network, operators may adjust settings and monitor alarms; maintenance teams inspect and repair equipment; managers set priorities and allocate budgets; suppliers provide equipment and spare parts; and users generate demand patterns and report problems.

Actors do not all have the same authority, information or degree of involvement. Nor must every actor fall within the system boundary chosen for a particular analysis. Some may belong to the environment while still having a significant influence on the system. Their role should therefore be identified, along with the reason for including them in the description.

The category is not useful in every kind of system. In systems without human or organisational agents—for example, some physical systems or automated processes—it may be unnecessary to force an actor-based description. The concept is most relevant where agents can make decisions or carry out intentional interventions.

### Capabilities: what a participant or component can do

A **capability** is the ability of an actor, component or organised combination of elements to perform an activity or fulfil a function under specified conditions.

In a water supply installation, an operator may be capable of interpreting control-system signals and following adjustment procedures. A maintenance team may have the technical capability to diagnose particular failures. A pump has a pumping capability determined by its characteristics and operating conditions; a control system may regulate its operation within defined limits.

Capability should be distinguished from observed activity. The fact that a person or component can perform a task does not mean that it is performing that task now. Likewise, a capability described in a procedure or specification may not be practically available if training, authorisation, tools or suitable conditions are missing.

Capabilities may reside in an individual element or depend on the combination of several. Maintaining service continuity, for example, may require operators who can interpret information, monitoring systems that provide it, and procedures that coordinate a response. No single participant necessarily provides everything required.

### Resources: the means available for action

**Resources** are the means that can be used to perform activities and sustain system operation. They may be material, energetic, financial, human, informational or temporal.

In a water network, physical resources include pumps, tools, spare parts and facilities; energy resources include the electricity supply; human resources include staff time and availability; and information resources include network plans, operating records, sensor data and technical documentation.

The existence of a resource does not guarantee that it is available for every purpose. It may already be committed to another activity, out of service, inaccessible when needed or subject to authorisation. A description should therefore distinguish between resources that exist, those that are available and those that can actually be mobilised for a particular task.

Resources should also not be confused with capabilities. Diagnostic equipment is a resource; knowing how to use it and interpret its results is a capability. A task may require both.

### Dependencies: what an activity relies on

A **dependency** exists when an activity, actor or component requires another element to provide something, perform a task or maintain a condition for the first to operate as intended.

For example, a repair may depend on spare parts being available, the maintenance team having access to the site, and the possibility of temporarily interrupting supply. The operation of a pumping station may depend on electricity, water availability and the correct functioning of its control systems.

Dependencies may be technical, operational, organisational, informational or external. Some are direct and visible; others become apparent only when an incident occurs. Their significance may also vary with the task and the circumstances.

Identifying dependencies helps explain why having a component or capability is not always enough to carry out an activity. Operation may rely on several contributions being available and coordinated.

### An integrated view of operation

Actors, capabilities, resources and dependencies are not separate categories to be examined in isolation. They are complementary dimensions of how an activity is supported within a system.

Imagine a failure in a main water pipe. Detecting it may require sensors and monitoring systems; interpreting it requires people with technical knowledge; planning the response requires decision-making authority and operational coordination; and carrying out the repair requires tools, spare parts, access and time. The response depends on these capabilities and resources being connected through appropriate procedures and responsibilities.

This example shows why it is not enough to say that an organisation has qualified staff or modern equipment. To understand its operational ability, we need to ask which tasks it can perform, with what means, under which conditions and with which dependencies.

### Bringing these dimensions into a system description

When analysing a system involving people or organisations, it may be useful to record:

- which actors are involved and what roles they perform;
- which capabilities matter to the activities under study;
- which resources those activities require and which are actually available;
- which people, components, services or conditions they depend on;
- which responsibilities, permissions or limits shape their involvement.

This does not mean producing an organisation chart or resource inventory by default. It means including the information needed to explain how activities are carried out and where critical limitations or dependencies may arise.

### Key idea

A system's operation may depend not only on its components, but also on the actors involved, the capabilities they bring, the resources they can mobilise and the dependencies that condition their actions.

### Reflection

Think of a service or installation you know. Choose an activity that is important to its operation and identify who needs to take part, which capabilities are required, what resources are used and which other people, components or conditions the activity depends on. What could prevent the activity from being carried out even if each element existed on its own?


## 1.5. Relationships, Interactions and System Structure

### A system is more than a collection of elements

Identifying a system's elements is necessary, but it is not enough to understand how the system operates. We also need to describe how those elements are related, what they exchange and how their relationships shape the behaviour of the whole.

In a water supply network, reservoirs, treatment facilities, pumps, pipes and points of use do not operate in isolation. Water flows between them; sensors transmit information; control systems adjust equipment; and maintenance activities affect infrastructure availability. The system is defined not only by its components, but also by the way they are connected and coordinated.

The **structure of a system** is the organisation of its elements and the relationships between them that matter to the analysis. It is not limited to physical layout: it also includes functional connections, information flows, operational dependencies and coordination mechanisms.

### Relationships and interactions

A **relationship** is a relevant link between two or more elements. It may represent a physical connection, a dependency, an authority relationship, an exchange of information or another association needed to describe the system.

An **interaction** refers to what happens when related elements act on or influence one another. It may involve transferring matter or energy, exchanging information, changing a condition or coordinating an activity.

In a water network, a pipe physically connects two parts of the infrastructure and allows water to flow. A sensor and a monitoring system have an informational relationship: the sensor provides measurements that the monitoring system uses to represent the installation's condition. When a reading triggers an adjustment to a pump or valve, we can observe an operational interaction between measurement, decision and action.

Not every relationship involves continuous interaction. A dependency may exist even if it becomes active only under particular circumstances. Conversely, a one-off interaction does not necessarily produce a lasting change in the system's structure.

### Flows, connections and coordination mechanisms

Relationships take different forms. Common examples include:

- **Physical flows:** the movement of water, materials, energy or products between elements.
- **Information flows:** the transmission of measurements, commands, alerts and records.
- **Functional connections:** links between components that work together to perform a function.
- **Operational dependencies:** conditions or contributions that one element requires from another.
- **Coordination and authority relationships:** the allocation of responsibilities, permissions and decisions among actors.

Several forms may overlap within the same system. A pumping station may be connected to the network through pipes, receive energy from an external infrastructure, send data to a control centre and depend on a maintenance team. Each link describes a different dimension of its integration into the whole.

To avoid ambiguity, a model should make clear what each connection represents. A line may indicate a physical flow, information transfer, dependency or influence; without an explanation, readers may assign it a meaning that was never intended.

### Structure shapes behaviour

A system's behaviour depends not only on the characteristics of its elements but also on how they are related. Changing a connection, interrupting a flow or modifying a coordination rule may alter the response of the whole, even when the individual components remain unchanged.

Imagine that a main pipe is taken out of service. The effect is not confined to the failed section: flow distribution may change, pressure may fall in some areas, and pumping adjustments or valve operations may become necessary. The scale and extent of these effects depend on the network structure, alternative connections and operating conditions.

A local change may also improve the performance of one component while making the overall system perform worse. Increasing the output of a pumping station, for instance, may raise energy consumption or exceed pressure limits elsewhere in the network. Understanding such consequences requires us to examine relevant relationships rather than focusing only on the component being changed.

### Feedback loops

Some system structures contain **feedback loops**: sequences of relationships in which the result of an activity feeds back into the operating conditions or decisions that follow.

In a water network, a sensor may detect a pressure drop; the control system interprets the signal and adjusts a pump; the change in pumping affects pressure, which is measured again. Information about the result thus informs a further decision or adjustment.

Feedback may help keep a variable within specified limits or, under other circumstances, amplify variations and produce unstable responses. Identifying a loop is not enough: its delays, limits, control rules and operating conditions also matter.

The term should not be applied to every return connection in a drawing. Feedback occurs when the result of a process influences that process again or affects its regulation.

### Representing structure without losing meaning

A diagram can help reveal connections and distinguish types of relationships, but every representation selects part of reality. An infrastructure schematic may emphasise physical flows and omit responsibilities; a control diagram may show signals and commands without representing water distribution; an organisational chart may show authority and coordination but not technical constraints.

A diagram should therefore be read in relation to its purpose, scope and legend. It is important to state what the nodes and connections represent, which relationships have been omitted, and whether arrows indicate flow direction, information transfer or influence.

The supporting diagram in this section uses a simplified water supply network to distinguish physical flows, information and control signals, and selected external dependencies. It is not an engineering drawing and does not attempt to represent every possible relationship in a real installation.

### Key idea

A system's structure consists of its elements and the relationships that connect them. Understanding those relationships—including flows, dependencies, coordination and feedback—is essential to explaining the behaviour of the whole.

### Reflection

Choose a system you know and draw its main elements and connections. Distinguish physical flows from information exchanges and dependencies. Which behaviour would be difficult to explain if you ignored one of those relationships? Which important connections are missing from your representation?

## 1.6. Open Systems, Changing Environments and Time

### Systems do not operate in isolation

A system can be bounded for the purpose of analysis without being isolated from everything outside that boundary. Many systems exchange matter, energy, information, resources or services with their environment. They are described as **open systems** when such exchanges are relevant to their operation.

A water supply network, for example, receives water from sources and energy to pump and treat it. It delivers water to users and organisations, generates information about its operation, and depends on external factors such as energy availability, weather conditions, applicable regulations, and the activities of suppliers and maintenance services.

A boundary helps us decide which elements to include in an analysis; it does not remove the relationships that cross that boundary. A sound description should therefore show both the system's internal organisation and the external exchanges and dependencies relevant to the question being studied.

### Inputs, outputs and exchanges

One way to begin describing the relationship between a system and its environment is to identify its **inputs**, **outputs** and **exchanges**. Inputs are what the system receives; outputs are what it delivers or produces; and exchanges also include flows that may move in both directions.

| Type of exchange | Example in a water supply network |
|---|---|
| Matter | Water abstracted, treated and distributed; sludge or waste from treatment |
| Energy | Electricity used by pumps and equipment |
| Information | Sensor data, demand forecasts, alerts and instructions |
| Resources and services | Spare parts, technical support and specialist staff |
| Outputs to the environment | Water supplied, service information and managed waste |

This classification supports analysis; it is not a universal list. In some systems, financial, regulatory, biological or social flows may also be essential. Selection depends on the purpose and level of detail of the study.

Nor should an input be mistaken for a sufficient condition for operation. Receiving energy, for instance, does not guarantee that a station can pump water if a pump has failed, a valve remains closed or no authorised personnel are available to intervene.

### The environment can change

A system's environment is not necessarily stable. Demand, resource availability, physical conditions, technologies, regulations and user expectations may all change.

In a water network, a prolonged drought may reduce water availability; a heatwave may increase demand; a regulatory change may require additional monitoring; and a power outage may temporarily limit pumping. Such external changes can alter the conditions under which the system operates even when its internal configuration has not changed.

Influence can also run in the opposite direction. A system's decisions and operations may affect its environment: infrastructure can influence land use, energy consumption or nearby ecosystems. The relationship between a system and its environment is therefore not always one-way.

### Time is part of the description

A description of a system at a particular moment does not necessarily explain how it operates over time. States, relationships, available resources and environmental conditions may change. To understand a dynamic system, we need to examine not only how it is configured but also how it evolves.

At least three time scales are often useful to distinguish:

- **Short term:** operational variations, daily demand cycles, responses to alarms or temporary interruptions.
- **Medium term:** maintenance, seasonal changes, equipment replacement, procedural adjustments and resource availability.
- **Long term:** infrastructure ageing, demographic change, climate trends, technological development and regulatory change.

These scales are not universal and have no fixed durations. A variation that is rapid for one infrastructure may be slow for another system. What matters is choosing a time window suited to the analytical question and not confusing a single observation with a persistent pattern.

### Disturbances, adaptation and evolution

Systems can respond in different ways to environmental changes or disturbances. They may absorb a variation without noticeably changing their operation, adjust how they operate, reorganise some elements or lose the ability to provide the expected service.

For example, in response to a temporary increase in demand, a network may use available pumping capacity, change operating set-points or manage pressure in particular areas. If high demand persists for years, more substantial measures may be needed, such as expanding infrastructure, introducing new sources or changing consumption patterns.

It is useful to distinguish an operational response from a more lasting transformation. Temporarily adjusting a pump is not the same as redesigning the network; likewise, a structural modification does not by itself guarantee successful adaptation. The response must be assessed against expected behaviour, constraints and the time horizon being considered.

### A snapshot and a moving picture of the system

A system can be described through a representation of its configuration at a particular moment, or through a representation showing how its conditions and behaviour change over time. Both perspectives are useful, but they answer different questions.

A snapshot of the water network may show which reservoirs, pumps and pipes are available today. A time-based description may show how reservoir levels, demand, energy use and incidents vary over a week or a season.

A temporal perspective also requires us to state when data were collected, which period they cover and which events might explain observed changes. Without this information, we may attribute to the system's structure what is actually due to a temporary condition or an external disturbance.

### Key idea

Open systems exchange with their environments and operate under conditions that may change. Understanding their behaviour requires more than describing components and relationships at one moment: it also requires attention to flows across the system boundary, environmental variation and the relevant time scale.

### Reflection

Choose a system you know. Identify what it receives from and delivers to its environment, which external conditions may change and over what time scales those changes occur. What aspects of its operation would remain unnoticed if you observed it at only one point in time?
